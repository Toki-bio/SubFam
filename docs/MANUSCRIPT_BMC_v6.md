## Abstract
**Background.** A transposable-element family, a satellite array or a multigene family can hold thousands to millions of related copies at every degree of divergence. Such a collection is beyond reading, and beyond the input size of the tools that follow (multiple aligners, tree builders, library curation). Clustering at a fixed identity suits one age of family at a time: a cutoff that separates the groups of a young family fragments an old family into singletons, and a cutoff that holds an old family together merges the groups of a young one.
**Results.** SubFam orders the copies along a k-mer guide tree, cuts the order into chunks of N neighbouring copies, aligns each chunk, calls a plurality consensus for each aligned chunk and aligns the consensuses. The output is a compact alignment, ordered by similarity, with a table of the copies behind every row; it needs neither a reference nor an identity cutoff, and N, the number of copies per row, is its one substantive parameter. On 3,000 mouse B2 SINE copies SubFam produced 60 rows in 19 s in which the lineages of the family appear as blocks, and on 30,000 copies 600 rows with the same structure; 2,222 5′-truncated human L1PA2–8 copies gave 111 rows that follow the age classes at the resolution whole copies allow. An ablation shows that the result rests on the combination of the steps: chunks of unordered copies are as impure as a single family-wide consensus. Identity clustering of the same copies with VSEARCH or MeShClust2 either fragments the old families into singletons or merges across their groups; on the young family it reaches the same purity as SubFam while leaving a tenth of the copies unplaced. With tree-partitioning ordering, 30,000 copies take seven minutes and 0.7 GB.
**Conclusions.** SubFam turns a pile of raw copies into an ordered, inspectable alignment that a person can read and a downstream tool can take. Its scope ends there: a row stands for a chunk of neighbouring copies, and the definition of subfamilies and the placement of orphan and truncated copies belong to the steps that follow.

**Keywords:** transposable elements; repeat families; consensus sequences; multiple sequence alignment; visualisation

## Background
Interspersed repeats, satellite arrays and multigene families contain thousands to millions of related copies, and the copies of one family span every degree of divergence, from near-identical recent insertions to relics at 30 % distance from their consensus. Such a collection is too large to inspect and too heterogeneous to group with one identity cutoff. Identity-based clustering (CD-HIT, VSEARCH, MMseqs2, MeShClust) returns either a real copy, which carries its own private mutations, or a cluster consensus whose content depends on where the cutoff fell, and leaves every copy below the cutoff on its own. Methods that define subfamilies statistically, from the genome-wide Alu analysis of Price et al. (2004) to COSEG, require every copy aligned to one reference over a shared window and set truncated copies aside. Library-curation tools such as TEtrimmer start from a genome and treat one locus at a time [docs/research/TETRIMMER.md]. Annotation assigns each copy its best-scoring library consensus, a label without a stated uncertainty.
Between the gold standard of manual curation and a researcher's computational analysis lies a practical gap: the heap of raw copies that neither a person nor a pipeline can take as it is. SubFam fills that gap, and only that gap. Its two objectives are (1) a visually observable presentation of a variety of similar sequences, at various degrees of intra- and inter-group variability, as similarity subgroups, and (2) an alignment proxy: a compact, ordered alignment of chunk consensuses that stands for thousands of repetitive sequences in the tools that cannot take them all. SubFam works without an identity cutoff and without a reference. A row is the consensus of a chunk of neighbouring copies; whether a row, or a block of rows, is a subfamily is a judgement for the researcher or for a dedicated method, and orphan and truncated copies are placed by the steps that follow.

## Implementation
**Algorithm.** (1) *Order.* All sequences are placed along a UPGMA tree built from the weighted Jaccard (Ružička) distance of their k-mer counts (default k = 6). At each merge the two subtrees are flipped so that their closest ends meet; the leaf order of this tree is a sequence in which neighbours are similar across subtree boundaries, which is what makes fixed windows along it meaningful. This step aligns nothing. The distance matrix agrees with a brute-force computation and the output is byte-identical across thread counts [docs/research/CODE_REVIEW.md]. (2) *Chunk.* The order is cut into round(n / N) consecutive blocks of near-equal size (default N = 50); every sequence belongs to exactly one chunk. (3) *Consensus.* Each chunk is aligned with MAFFT, chunks in parallel, and a plurality consensus is called for each aligned chunk with the scoring of EMBOSS cons (EDNAFULL): the top-scoring residue of a column is written when its positive-scoring matches reach p × n (default p = 0.36); a base supported by half of the sequences or fewer is written in lower case, and the case is kept through the final alignment. With `-c` the threshold is p × the number of sequences that span the column (at least 3), so that the consensus of a chunk of truncated copies extends as far as its copies do. (4) *Align.* The chunk consensuses are aligned with MAFFT L-INS-i (`--auto` above 2,000 consensuses). Outputs: the consensuses, their alignment (FASTA and MSF), a table of copy, chunk and strand, and a label-free table of chunk statistics (size, consensus length, share of weakly supported bases, agreement of the members with the consensus, lowest member identity, gap share). Two options lead back to the original loci: `-e` writes the original sequences of chosen chunks to separate FASTA files, and `-g` reports the composition of every chunk by groups the user supplies. Mixed-strand input is handled by `-r` (strand-independent k-mers; each copy is oriented like its neighbour in the order). Grouping of the rows (by diagnostic columns, by a tree cut, by annotation) is the next step and is left to it.
**Parameter.** N is the resolution in copies: a group needs roughly N copies to obtain a row of its own. The default of 50 is the smallest chunk that still keeps the properties of a sequence group and gives a plurality consensus with statistical weight: at p = 0.36 a base needs 18 agreeing copies, enough for the private mutations of single copies to cancel out, whereas chunks of 20 call a consensus on 8 copies and follow the noise of diverged or damaged copies. In the simulations the rows of N = 50 represent their copies more closely than those of N = 20 (a copy's own row is its best-matching row for 98.1 against 95.0 % of copies in the young family and 57.5 against 48.9 % in the old one [benchmark/multiseed]), while N = 20 recovers rare subfamilies of 40–60 copies that N = 50 merges, and N = 100 dilutes the rare groups further. N = 20 is the setting for small or rare groups, N = 50 the default for a family. On the B2 example the purity of the chunks against annotation labels moves by 0.05 over N = 20–100, by at most 0.04 over k = 4–10, by 0.02 between random samples of the copies, and stays the same for p = 0.25–0.5, since p shapes the consensus and leaves the chunk membership unchanged [benchmark/sine_b2/sensitivity.tsv].
**Input size.** The examples use 3,000 and 30,000 copies for a reason: at N = 50 they give 60 and 600 rows, and 600 rows is about the most an alignment viewer and a reader can take in at once. A larger input brings no further view: copies from many diverged groups distort one alignment, and copies that form close groups compress to the same few hundred consensuses, so a bigger sample is better handled as a larger N, a random subsample, or the cascade of the Discussion.
**Scale.** The k-mer ordering holds a dense n × n float32 matrix (0.8 GB at 10,000 copies, 2.2 GB at 20,000); the script warns above 20,000, and PartTree ordering (`-P`) replaces the matrix: 10,000 and 20,000 simulated copies took 65 and 173 s at 104 and 194 MB, against 75 and 247 s at 839 and 2,216 MB for the k-mer path, with all eight simulated sources recovered either way and chunk purity 0.89–0.90 against 0.92–0.95 [docs/SCALING.md].
**Equivalence and tests.** The consensus and MSF writers reproduce EMBOSS cons and seqret byte for byte [tests/check_equivalence.sh]. The consensus and chunk-statistics code agrees with independent reimplementations on 8,000 random alignments under two awk implementations, and the chunk splitter places every sequence in exactly one chunk for every input size from 1 to 30 and N from 2 to 10 (code review of 2026-10-11; [AUTHOR: add as tests/ or cite the review file]).
**Presentation of the examples.** Every example alignment (`examples/`) is one MAFFT alignment of three kinds of row: *class consensuses* (`CLASSCONS_<class>`: the plurality consensus of the real member copies of a labelled class, the best any method could reach given the labels, and distinct from the truth), *published consensuses* (Dfam, Price et al. 2004), and the SubFam rows, each named `<chunk>|<class>|p<purity>`, purity being the fraction of the chunk's copies in its majority class. Labels (RepeatMasker classes, published subfamilies) serve to colour and to score; the method itself uses none of them. The identity of every SubFam row to the head of its group is in `<file>.rows.tsv`.

## Results and discussion
### Example 1: mouse B2 SINEs (Figure 1, Figure 2)
Every RepeatMasker B2_Mm1a, B2_Mm1t, B2_Mm2, B3 and B3A copy on chr19 of mm39 (≥ 170 bp, free of N, on the element strand) was taken: 5,972 copies; a random 3,000 (seed 1) were run with `-n 50`, giving 60 rows in 19 s [benchmark/sine_b2/README.md]. Grouped by RepeatMasker class, with each class's member consensus and the Dfam consensus as heads (Figure 1), the 60 rows have a median identity of 0.959 to the head of their own class (minimum 0.836), and 5 rows lie closer to another head. The k-mer order separates the B3 and B2 lineages into blocks of chunks (B3/B3A: chunks 1–21 and 46–60; B2: chunks 22–45). Inside the B2 block the three Repbase names interleave, and B2_Mm1t, whose consensus differs from B2_Mm1a at 2 of 193 positions, has no rows of its own; the old B3A class (542 copies) shares its rows with B3. The alignment shows this structure; the question of whether the Repbase names or an indel-based classification of the family (Vassetzky et al. 2021) fit the copies better belongs to another paper [AUTHOR: co-author of that classification; the comparison is in the supplement, `benchmark/sine_b2/README.md`].
*From chaos to order (Figure 2).* To make the method visible, 390 of the copies (a stratified random sample over four groups of the family, seed 1, shuffled) were aligned together with the 20 chunk consensuses SubFam built from them (`-n 20`). In input order the copies are speckle; in SubFam's order the groups appear as blocks with their shared indels, and the 20 consensuses carry the same structure in 20 rows [docs/figures/sketch_chaos_to_order_b2.png; `benchmark/sine_b2/figure_chaos_to_order.py`].
*Limit.* The oldest copies of the family, about 70 % identical to their own consensus, mix across chunks at N = 50, and the alignment shows that mixing as it is. At 25–30 % divergence between copies this is the limit of every copy-level method.

### Example 2: 5′-truncated L1 copies (Supplementary Figure 1)
2,222 RepeatMasker L1PA2–L1PA8 copies of at least 1 kb on hg38 chr4 (mean length 3.3 kb; over 90 % of L1 copies are 5′-truncated), run with `-n 20 -c`: 111 rows in 6 min 31 s on 8 threads [benchmark/line_real/README.md]. The rows reach as far 5′ as their copies do (`-c`); against the head of their RepeatMasker class they have a median identity of 0.993 (minimum 0.955), and 17 of 111 rows lie closer to a neighbouring class's head. The L1PA classes are consecutive age classes of one lineage whose consensuses differ at 8–31 of about 900 positions; the chunks follow them at the resolution of neighbouring classes (PA2 + PA3, PA4–PA6, PA7 + PA8). *Limit.* The whole-copy k-mer distance responds to copy length as well as to sequence: chunks are nearly length-homogeneous (within-chunk length CV 0.05, against 0.59 for random chunks), so one class spreads over several rows by length, and the tree built from the 3′ region that every copy shares differs from the whole-copy tree [benchmark/line_real/README.md §4–5, Supplementary Table]. For 5′-truncated families the recommended use is to run SubFam on the region the copies share, or on the long copies, and to place the fragments afterwards.

### Example 3: young Alu loci (Supplementary Figure 2)
316 Sanger-sequenced polymorphic Alu loci (Konkel et al. 2015), cut to the Alu body and run with `-n 20`, give 16 rows; the Price et al. (2004) consensuses for AluY, AluYa5 and AluYb8 head their groups, with a median identity of rows to head of 1.000 (minimum 0.996) [examples/konkel_alu_n20.rows.tsv]. Seven of the 16 rows are distinct sequences: in a young family several chunks of one group yield the same consensus, which is the price of fixed chunks (Discussion). The references are the consensuses that labelled these copies, so this example illustrates the output rather than validating it. ### Example 4: the same family at 30,000 copies (stress case)
Full-length B2 copies of mm39 chromosomes 1–3 (41,989; the rule of Example 1), a random 30,000 (seed 1), `-n 50`: 600 rows, 588 of them distinct, in 6.3 min at 4.1 GB with the k-mer order (13 min with the CPU shared) and in 7 min at 0.7 GB with `-P`. Chunk purity against the RepeatMasker class is 0.805 (0.756 with `-P`; 0.804 and 0.809 for two further random samples of the same copies), against 0.793 at 3,000 copies, so the picture of Example 1 holds at ten times the size. The Dfam consensuses are in the rows at identity 1.000 (B2_Mm1a), 0.995 (B2_Mm2), 0.984 (B2_Mm1t), 0.943 (B3) and 0.889 (B3A), the last two being the old classes whose copies are 25–30 % diverged; 35 of the 60 rows of the chr19 run have a row at ≥ 0.95 among the 600, the rest being B3/B3A rows [benchmark/sine_b2/README.md, stress case; `score_big.py`]. For comparison, 30,000 full-length Alu copies of hg38 chr1 (of 76,301) give 600 rows in 12 min (7 min with `-P`) in which 25 of the 26 Dfam human Alu families with 50 or more expected copies in the sample are present at ≥ 0.95 identity (21 at 0.98; median best identity 0.994), and 9 of the 10 families with fewer than 10 expected copies [benchmark/alu_hg38/]. In SubFam's row order the 600 rows form one block of B2 chunks (Mm2, Mm1t and Mm1a interleaved, the 11-bp deletion of the B2 lineage visible as a shared gap) flanked by B3/B3A chunks on both sides, the structure of the 60-row run at ten times the resolution (Supplementary Figure 4). At `-n 20` the same 30,000 B2 copies give 1,500 rows (1,486 distinct) at purity 0.826 in 15 min. On the Alu sample all 74 Price et al. (2004) subfamilies expected to have 50 or more copies in the sample are present at ≥ 0.95 identity (61 at 0.98), 88 of 90 with 10–50 expected copies and 45 of 49 with fewer; recovery falls with the expected copy number, as a chunk needs about N copies of a group [benchmark/alu_hg38/README.md].

### What each step contributes (Table 2)
Each step of SubFam has a counterpart elsewhere: guide-tree leaf orders (MAFFT `--reorder`), the decomposition of a guide tree into subsets (PASTA, UPP), family consensus builders (RepeatModeler's Refiner), per-cluster consensuses (VSEARCH `--consout`). The compact, ordered alignment with a provenance table comes from their combination, and the ablation measures the share of each [benchmark/ablation/README.md]. On the 3,000 B2 copies with N = 50 (purity against the RepeatMasker label; the label-free chunk statistics rank the variants in the same order):

| variant | rows | columns | purity |
|---|---|---|---|
| SubFam (k-mer order with closest-ends flip, chunks, consensus, alignment) | 60 | 223 | 0.79 |
| ordering removed: shuffled input, same chunks | 60 | 237 | 0.43 |
| ordering removed: genomic order | 60 | 242 | 0.43 |
| MAFFT guide-tree order in place of the k-mer order | 60 | 223 | 0.73 |
| k-mer UPGMA order without the closest-ends flip | 60 | 234 | 0.77 |
| chunking removed: one consensus of all copies | 1 | 194 | 0.42 |
| consensus removed: MAFFT alignment of all 3,000 copies | 3,000 | 2,781 | – |

Chunks of unordered copies are as impure as a single family consensus; the generic guide-tree order supplies most of the gain, the k-mer order with the flip the rest; with the consensus step removed, the researcher is left with the 3,000-row alignment.

With either tool, every cutoff on the old families gives one of two outcomes: a low cutoff merges across classes (L1 at 0.80: three groups hold 96 % of the copies at purity 0.36), a high cutoff fragments the set (B2 at 0.80: 1,906 of 3,000 copies are singletons). On the young Alu loci a working cutoff exists, and vsearch at 0.95 matches SubFam in purity (0.94 against 0.92) over 89 % of the copies; there SubFam adds completeness and the order. Caveats: purity favours small groups, hence the coverage column; the labels are annotations rather than truth; `cluster_fast` is greedy; each family is one chromosome. COSEG, run once on the Konkel loci, separated AluYb8 from the rest and left AluYa5 with AluY; at matched granularity it scored above SubFam's fixed chunks on V-measure, the chunks being over-split by design [benchmark/alu_konkel/README.md].

### Simulation (Supplementary Figure 3)
Simulation supplies what real data withhold: a known truth across family ages. With eight subfamilies at 3, 8 and 15 % divergence (eight replicates each), SubFam `-n 20` recovered all eight source consensuses in every run with 100 rows for 2,000 copies, whereas the VSEARCH identity that recovers the sources moved with age (0.90–0.95 for the young family, 0.70 for the old one), and even the best identity chosen per replicate with knowledge of the truth recovered 5.25 of 8 in the old family [benchmark/multiseed]. In a harder simulation (CpG decay, source elements, mixed ages, truncated copies) SubFam recovered 6.3–7.8 of 8 at 100 rows with identity measured over non-CpG columns; over all columns, CpG decay keeps nearly every consensus of every method below 99 % identity to the master [benchmark/hard]. In the simple simulation 10 of the 40 rows at N = 50 are distinct, an abundant subfamily filling several chunks with one consensus. Rows represent their copies closely in young and middle families and less closely in old ones (a copy's own row was its best-matching row for 98.1, 87.3 and 57.5 % of copies). The simulations are star-like and of our own design and serve as a supplement.

### What SubFam gives
A readable, ordered alignment that stands for the collection, every row traceable to its copies, built without a cutoff and without a reference. The ablation locates the contribution in the combination of the steps rather than in any one of them, and in the fixed chunk, which trades redundancy (several rows for an abundant group) for freedom from a cutoff.
### The steps that follow
Groups are defined downstream, on the compact alignment, where the methods that need a small input become applicable: a search for diagnostic columns (a column at which a block of rows is fixed for one base that the other rows lack), a tree or network of the rows, a curated library built from chosen rows and their member copies (`-e`), or a comparison of the chunks with a classification of the user's own (`-g`). Each of these is a method with its own test; this paper describes the preparation of their input. In our own analyses of SINE families the chunk consensuses served as the material from which subfamilies were delimited and their consensuses chosen [AUTHOR: cite only the two or three papers whose Methods name SubFam; candidates: Vassetzky et al. 2021 (already cited for B2), Kosushkin et al. 2022 Can, Ustyantsev et al. 2024 Ere, Borodulina et al. 2025, Ustyantsev et al. 2026].
### Cascaded use
When the copies come from subfamilies of different age and size, one N serves them unequally: a young, abundant subfamily fills many near-identical rows while an old or rare one is diluted across mixed chunks. SubFam can then be run in cascade with sequential depletion: the rows of a first run that the researcher recognises as one group are taken out together with their copies (`-e` gives the copies of chosen chunks; the remainder is the next input), and SubFam is run again on what is left with settings adjusted to it, a smaller N for the rare groups, `-c` or a shared region for truncated ones, a different k for old ones. Each round keeps every copy accounted for, and the rows of all rounds can be aligned together at the end. This is a use of the tool, with the grouping decision made by the researcher between rounds.
### Relationship to existing tools
Identity clustering returns one representative per cluster and leaves the clusters unordered; COSEG and Price et al. define subfamilies on full-length copies aligned to one reference; TEtrimmer curates a library per locus from a genome; PASTA and MAGUS decompose a guide tree in order to align everything, where SubFam collapses the subsets and aligns the consensuses [docs/research/RELATED_WORK.md]. Each answers a different question; SubFam serves as a step of input preparation for them, reducing a collection of copies to an ordered set of consensuses they can take.
### Limitations
A chunk has a fixed size: an abundant group fills several rows with near-identical consensuses, a chunk can straddle two groups, and N has to be chosen for the rarest group of interest. Rows represent old families less closely than young ones. The whole-copy k-mer distance responds to copy length, so mixed-length input (5′-truncated LINEs) sorts partly by length; the remedy is to run on the shared region or on the long copies. Memory is quadratic without `-P`. The labels used to score the examples are annotations or published consensuses, and each real example is one chromosome of one genome.

## Conclusions
SubFam turns a collection of related copies that is too large to read into a compact, ordered alignment of chunk consensuses, with every row traceable to its copies, built without an identity cutoff and without a reference. On real SINE and LINE copies the rows carry the known consensuses of the family and show its lineages as blocks; removing any step of the method loses the result, and identity clustering of the same copies either fragments old families or merges young groups. The output is the input that curation, subfamily definition and phylogenetic analysis need and cannot take from the raw copies; those steps, and the definition of subfamilies, remain the researcher's.
