## Abstract
**Background.** A transposable-element family, a satellite array or a multigene family can hold thousands to millions of related copies at every degree of divergence. Such a collection is too large to read and to give to the tools that follow (aligners, tree builders, library curation). Clustering at a fixed identity suits one age of family at a time: a cutoff that separates the groups of a young family fragments an old family into singletons, and a cutoff that holds an old family together merges the groups of a young one.
**Results.** SubFam orders the copies along a k-mer guide tree, cuts the order into chunks of N neighbouring copies, aligns each chunk, calls a plurality consensus for each aligned chunk and aligns the consensuses. The output is a compact alignment, ordered by similarity, with a table of the copies behind every row; it needs neither a reference nor an identity cutoff, and its main parameter is N, the number of copies per row. A family of up to millions of copies can be run on a random sample: at the default N, about 30,000 copies give some 600 rows, about as many as a reader can take in at once. On mouse B2 SINEs the B2 and B3 lineages appear as blocks of rows; on human Alu the published subfamilies with enough copies in the sample appear as rows; on 5′-truncated LINE-1 copies the chunks mix only neighbouring age classes. An ablation shows that the result rests on ordering and chunking together: chunks of unordered copies are as impure as a single family-wide consensus. Identity clustering with VSEARCH or MeShClust2 gives no cutoff at which the groups of the old families are both pure and cover all copies; on the young family VSEARCH matches SubFam's purity with a tenth of the copies in groups under ten.
**Conclusions.** SubFam turns a pile of raw copies into an ordered, inspectable alignment that a person can read and a downstream tool can take. Defining subfamilies and placing orphan and truncated copies are left to the steps that follow.

**Keywords:** transposable elements; repeat families; consensus sequences; multiple sequence alignment; visualisation

## Background
Interspersed repeats (short and long interspersed elements, SINEs and LINEs), satellite arrays and multigene families contain thousands to millions of related copies, and the copies of one family span every degree of divergence, from near-identical recent insertions to relics at 30 % distance from their consensus. Such a collection is too large to inspect and too heterogeneous to group with one identity cutoff. Identity-based clustering (CD-HIT [1], VSEARCH [2], MMseqs2 [3, 4], MeShClust [5]) returns either a real copy, which carries its own private mutations, or a cluster consensus whose content depends on where the cutoff fell, and leaves every copy below the cutoff on its own. Methods that define subfamilies statistically, from the genome-wide Alu analysis of Price et al. [6] to COSEG [7], require every copy aligned to one reference over a shared window and set truncated copies aside. Library-curation tools such as TEtrimmer [8] start from a genome and refine one family consensus at a time. Annotation assigns each copy its best-scoring library consensus, a label without a stated uncertainty.
Between the gold standard of manual curation and a researcher's computational analysis lies a practical gap: the heap of raw copies that neither a person nor a pipeline can take as it is. SubFam fills that gap, and only that gap. Its two objectives are (1) a visually observable presentation of a variety of similar sequences, at various degrees of intra- and inter-group variability, as similarity subgroups, and (2) an alignment proxy: a compact, ordered alignment of chunk consensuses that stands for thousands of repetitive sequences in the tools that cannot take them all. SubFam works without an identity cutoff and without a reference. A row is the consensus of a chunk of neighbouring copies; whether a row, or a block of rows, is a subfamily is a judgement for the researcher or for a dedicated method, and orphan and truncated copies are placed by the steps that follow.

## Implementation
**Algorithm.** (1) *Order.* All sequences are placed along a UPGMA (unweighted pair group method with arithmetic mean) tree built from the weighted Jaccard (Ružička) distance of their k-mer counts (default k = 6), computed in C (`kmer_order.c`, compiled on each run; an embedded Python version with byte-identical output is the fallback where no compiler exists). At each merge the two subtrees are turned so that their closest ends meet (of the four pairs of end sequences, the pair at the smallest distance is placed side by side); the leaf order of this tree is a sequence in which neighbours are similar across subtree boundaries, which is what makes fixed windows along it meaningful. This step aligns nothing. The distance matrix agrees with a brute-force computation and the output is byte-identical across thread counts. (2) *Chunk.* The order is cut into round(n / N) consecutive blocks (at least one) of near-equal size (default N = 50); every sequence belongs to exactly one chunk. (3) *Consensus.* Each chunk is aligned with MAFFT [9], chunks in parallel, and a plurality consensus is called for each aligned chunk with the scoring of EMBOSS cons [10] (EDNAFULL): the top-scoring residue of a column is written when the sequences whose residue scores positively against it (ambiguity codes included, gaps never) number at least p × n (default p = 0.36); a base supported by half of the sequences or fewer is written in lower case, and the case is kept through the final alignment. With `-c` the threshold is p × the number of sequences that span the column (at least 3), so that the consensus of a chunk of truncated copies extends as far as its copies do; the lower-case rule still counts all sequences of the chunk, so the parts that only a few copies reach are written in lower case. (4) *Align.* The chunk consensuses are aligned with MAFFT: L-INS-i with full iterative refinement up to 300 consensuses, L-INS-i with two refinement rounds up to 1,000, and MAFFT's automatic mode beyond. The rows of the alignment are in MAFFT's guide-tree order (`--reorder`); the consensus file and the chunk table keep the k-mer order. Outputs: the consensuses, their alignment (FASTA and multiple sequence format, MSF), a table of copy, chunk and strand, and a label-free table of chunk statistics (size, consensus length, share of lower-case bases and, over the core columns where at least half of the chunk has a base, the agreement of the members with each column's most frequent base, their median and lowest identity to those bases, and the share of gappy columns). Two options lead back to the original loci: `-e` writes the original sequences of chosen chunks to separate FASTA files, and `-g` reports the composition of every chunk by groups the user supplies. Mixed-strand input is handled by `-r` (strand-independent k-mers; each copy is oriented like its neighbour in the order). Grouping of the rows (by diagnostic columns, by a tree cut, by annotation) is the next step and is left to it.
**Parameter.** N is the resolution in copies: a group needs roughly N copies to obtain a row of its own. The default N = 50 gives a plurality consensus with statistical weight: at p = 0.36 a base needs 18 agreeing copies, enough for the private mutations of single copies to cancel out, whereas chunks of 20 call a consensus on 8 copies. A smaller N gives more rows, each purer against the labels but resting on fewer copies. In the simulations the rows of N = 50 represent their copies more closely than those of N = 20 in the young and the old family (a copy's own row is its best-matching row for 98.1 against 95.0 % and 57.5 against 48.9 % of copies) and equally in the middle one (87.3 against 88.0 %), while N = 20 recovers rare subfamilies of 40–60 copies that N = 50 merges. N = 20 is the setting for small or rare groups, N = 50 the default for a family. On the B2 example the purity of the chunks against annotation labels falls from 0.81 to 0.76 over N = 20–100, by at most 0.04 over k = 4–10, by 0.02 between random samples of the copies, and stays the same for p = 0.25–0.5, since p shapes the consensus and leaves the chunk membership unchanged.
**Input size.** At N = 50, 3,000 copies give 60 rows and 30,000 copies give 600; in the author's experience about 600 rows is the practical limit for reading an alignment in a viewer. A larger family is therefore better handled as a random sample, with a larger N, or by the cascade of the Discussion. SubFam does not sample by itself: the sample is drawn before the run with any sampling tool (for example `seqkit sample`; the 30,000-copy samples of Example 4 were drawn at random with fixed seeds), and its size, not that of the whole family, sets time and memory. A group needs about N copies in the sample to obtain a row of its own, so rare groups are recovered less often (Example 4).
**Scale.** The k-mer ordering holds a dense n × n float32 matrix of 4·n² bytes (0.4, 1.6 and 3.6 GB at 10,000, 20,000 and 30,000 copies; the measured peak memory of whole runs was 0.8, 2.2 and 4.1–4.3 GB). On Linux SubFam compares the matrix size with the free memory and stops with a message when it does not fit; where free memory cannot be read it warns above 20,000 sequences. PartTree ordering [11] (`-P`) replaces the matrix and needs far less memory, at a cost in chunk purity: 10,000 and 20,000 simulated copies took 65 and 173 s at 104 and 194 MB, against 75 and 247 s at 839 and 2,216 MB for the k-mer path, with all eight simulated sources recovered either way and chunk purity 0.89–0.90 against 0.92–0.95; on 30,000 real Alu copies the purity fell from 0.472 to 0.400 (Example 4). `-P` is therefore meant only for inputs whose matrix does not fit in memory.
**Equivalence and tests.** The consensus and MSF writers reproduce EMBOSS cons and seqret byte for byte (`tests/check_equivalence.sh`: 3,600 consensus runs and 80 MSF files, no difference). The C and Python orderings give byte-identical orders on 292 synthetic cases; 26 robustness cases (awkward or malformed inputs, determinism across runs and thread counts) either keep every input record or stop with a message, and `tests/check_inputs.sh` confirms that malformed input (duplicate or reserved ids, empty records, protein) is refused.
**Presentation of the examples.** The example alignments (Additional file 1) are MAFFT alignments of up to three kinds of row: *class consensuses* (`CLASSCONS_<class>`: the plurality consensus of the real member copies of a labelled class, the best any method could reach given the labels, and distinct from the truth), *published consensuses* (Dfam [12], Price et al. [6]), and the SubFam rows, each named `<chunk>|<class>|p<purity>`, purity being the fraction of the chunk's copies in its majority class. Labels (RepeatMasker [13] classes, published subfamilies) serve to colour and to score; the method itself uses none of them. The identity of every SubFam row to the head of its group is in `<file>.rows.tsv`. Exceptions: the Alu-loci alignment has published heads only, the 30,000-copy B2 alignment holds the SubFam rows only, and the L1 class consensuses are built from at most 60 random members of each class.

## Results and discussion
### Example 1: mouse B2 SINEs (Figure 1, Figure 2)
Every RepeatMasker B2_Mm1a, B2_Mm1t, B2_Mm2, B3 and B3A copy on chr19 of mm39 [14] (≥ 170 bp, free of N, on the element strand) was taken: 5,972 copies; a random 3,000 (seed 1) were run with `-n 50`, giving 60 rows in 19 s. Grouped by RepeatMasker class, with each class's member consensus and the Dfam consensus as heads (Figure 1), the 60 rows have a median identity of 0.959 to the head of their own class (minimum 0.836), and 5 rows lie closer to another head. The k-mer order separates the B3 and B2 lineages into blocks of chunks (B3/B3A: chunks 1–21 and 46–60; B2: chunks 22–45). Inside the B2 block the three Repbase [15] names interleave: B2_Mm1t, whose Dfam consensus differs from that of B2_Mm1a at 3 of 193 positions (with an N at a fourth), is the majority class of 6 rows, 5 of them at purity ≤ 0.66 and 3 closer to the B2_Mm1a or B2_Mm2 head. The old B3A class (542 copies) is the majority of 9 rows, mostly impure (purity 0.50–0.92) and interleaved with B3 rows. The alignment shows this structure; the question of whether the Repbase names or an indel-based classification of the family (Vassetzky et al. [16]) fit the copies better belongs to another paper; the two classifications are compared in Additional file 2 [AUTHOR: state that you co-authored that classification].
*From chaos to order (Figure 2).* To make the method visible, 390 of the copies (a stratified random sample over four groups of the family, seed 1, shuffled) were aligned together with the 20 chunk consensuses SubFam built from them (`-n 20`). In input order the copies are speckle; in SubFam's order the groups appear as blocks with their shared indels, and the 20 consensuses carry the same structure in 20 rows.
*Limit.* The oldest copies of the family, about 30 % diverged from their own consensus, mix across chunks at N = 50, and the alignment shows that mixing as it is; neither SubFam nor identity clustering (Table 2) separates them.

### Example 2: 5′-truncated LINE-1 (L1) copies (Additional file 3)
2,222 RepeatMasker L1PA2–L1PA8 copies of at least 1 kb on hg38 chr4 (mean length 3.3 kb; over 90 % of L1 copies are 5′-truncated [17]), run with `-n 20 -c`: 111 rows in 6 min 31 s on 8 threads. The rows reach as far 5′ as their copies do (`-c`); against the head of their RepeatMasker class they have a median identity of 0.993 (minimum 0.955), and 17 of 111 rows lie closer to another class's head, 16 of them to a neighbouring class's. The L1PA classes are consecutive age classes of one lineage [17, 18] whose consensuses differ at 8–31 of about 900 positions; the chunks mix neighbouring classes, and 92.6 % of the copies fall within one class of their chunk's majority class. *Limit.* The whole-copy k-mer distance responds to copy length as well as to sequence: chunks are nearly length-homogeneous (within-chunk length coefficient of variation, CV, 0.05, against 0.59 for random chunks), so one class spreads over several rows by length, and the tree built from the 3′ region that every copy shares differs from the whole-copy tree (Additional file 4). For 5′-truncated families the recommended use is to run SubFam on the region the copies share, or on the long copies, and to place the fragments afterwards.

### Example 3: young Alu loci (Additional file 5)
316 Sanger-sequenced polymorphic Alu loci (Konkel et al. [19]), cut to the Alu body and run with `-n 20`, give 16 rows; the Price et al. [6] consensuses for AluY, AluYa5 and AluYb8 head their groups, with a median identity of rows to head of 1.000 (minimum 0.996). Seven of the 16 rows are distinct sequences: in a young family several chunks of one group yield the same consensus, which is the price of fixed chunks (Discussion). The references are the consensuses that labelled these copies, so this example illustrates the output rather than validating it. A run on 30,000 Alu copies of hg38 chr1 is part of Example 4.

### Example 4: the same family at 30,000 copies (stress case)
B2-family copies of at least 170 bp on mm39 chromosomes 1–3 (41,989; the rule of Example 1), a random 30,000 (seed 1), `-n 50`: 600 rows, 588 of them distinct, in 13 min at 4.1 GB with the k-mer order on a shared processor (6.3 min on an idle one for two further random samples of the same copies) and in 7 min at 0.7 GB with `-P`, also on the shared processor (Additional file 6). Chunk purity against the RepeatMasker class is 0.805 (0.756 with `-P`; 0.804 and 0.809 for the two further samples), against 0.793 at 3,000 copies. The Dfam consensuses are in the rows at identity 1.000 (B2_Mm1a), 0.995 (B2_Mm2), 0.984 (B2_Mm1t), 0.943 (B3; 0.952 and 0.954 in the two further samples) and 0.889 (B3A), the last two being the old classes whose copies are about 30 % diverged from their consensus. All 24 B2 rows of the chr19 run (chunks 22–45) and 11 of its 27 B3 rows have a row at ≥ 0.95 identity among the 600, and none of its 9 B3A rows does (35 of 60; 37–40 in the other runs). For comparison, 30,000 full-length Alu copies of hg38 chr1 (of 76,301) give 600 rows in 12 min at 4.3 GB on the shared processor (chunk purity against the RepeatMasker name 0.472; with `-P` 6.7 min at 0.8 GB and purity 0.400) in which 25 of the 26 Dfam human Alu families with 50 or more expected copies in the sample are present at ≥ 0.95 identity (21 at 0.98; median best identity 0.994), and 9 of the 10 families with fewer than 10 expected copies. In chunk order the 600 rows form one block of B2 chunks (Mm2, Mm1t and Mm1a interleaved, the 11-bp deletion of the B2 lineage visible as a shared gap) flanked by B3/B3A chunks on both sides, the structure of the 60-row run in ten times as many rows (Additional file 7). At `-n 20` the same 30,000 B2 copies give 1,500 rows (1,486 distinct) at purity 0.826 in 15 min, the ordering on the shared processor. On the Alu sample all 74 Price et al. [6] subfamilies expected to have 50 or more copies in the sample are present at ≥ 0.95 identity (61 at 0.98), 88 of 90 with 10–50 expected copies and 45 of 49 with fewer; recovery falls with the expected copy number, as a chunk needs about N copies of a group.

### What each step contributes (Table 1)
Each step of SubFam has a counterpart elsewhere: guide-tree leaf orders (MAFFT `--reorder`), the decomposition of a guide tree into subsets (PASTA [20], UPP [21]), family consensus builders (RepeatModeler's Refiner [22]), per-cluster consensuses (VSEARCH `--consout`). The compact, ordered alignment with a provenance table comes from their combination, and the ablation measures the share of each. On the 3,000 B2 copies with N = 50 the mean lowest member identity of the chunks, a statistic that uses no labels, separates the ordered variants (0.71–0.72) from the unordered ones (0.59–0.60) and from a single consensus (0.50) (Table 1).

Chunks of unordered copies are as impure as a single family consensus; the generic guide-tree order supplies most of the gain, the k-mer order with the flip the rest; with the consensus step removed, the researcher is left with the 3,000-row alignment.

On the old families no cutoff of either tool gives groups that are both pure and cover the copies: a low cutoff merges across classes (L1 at 0.80: three groups hold 96 % of the copies at purity 0.36), a high cutoff fragments the set (B2 at 0.80: 1,906 of 3,000 copies are singletons), and an intermediate one does both in part (B2 at 0.60: 13 % of the copies are singletons and 63 % are in groups of at least 10, at purity 0.67). The comparison depends on the labels: scored against the indel-based classification of Vassetzky et al. [16] instead of RepeatMasker, the vsearch groups of B2 are purer than the SubFam chunks (0.85 at 0.60 over 63 % of the copies and 0.95 at 0.70 over 40 %, against 0.77 for SubFam over all copies). On the young Alu loci a working cutoff exists, and vsearch at 0.95 matches SubFam in purity (0.94 against 0.92) over 89 % of the copies, the rest being in groups of 2–9 copies or singletons; there SubFam adds completeness and the order. Caveats: purity favours small groups, hence the coverage column; the labels are annotations rather than truth; `cluster_fast` is greedy; the B2 and L1 copies each come from one chromosome. COSEG, run on 295 of the Konkel loci at three minimum group sizes, separated AluYb8 from the rest and left AluYa5 with AluY; at matched granularity it scored above SubFam's fixed chunks on V-measure, the chunks being over-split by design.

### Simulation (Additional file 8)
Simulation supplies what real data withhold: a known truth across family ages. With eight subfamilies at 3, 8 and 15 % divergence (eight replicates each), SubFam `-n 20` recovered all eight source consensuses in every run with 100 rows for 2,000 copies, whereas the VSEARCH identity that worked best moved with age (0.90–0.95 for the young family, 0.85 for the middle one, 0.70 for the old one), and even the best identity chosen per replicate with knowledge of the truth recovered 5.25 of 8 in the old family. In a harder simulation (CpG decay, source elements, mixed ages, truncated copies) SubFam recovered 6.3–7.8 of 8 at 100 rows with identity measured over non-CpG columns; over all columns, CpG decay pulls the consensuses away from the master as divergence grows, and in the old family no method recovers any source. In one run of the simple simulation (middle family, seed 1) 10 of the 40 rows at N = 50 are distinct: each abundant subfamily fills several chunks with one consensus. Rows represent their copies closely in young and middle families and less closely in old ones (N = 50, seeds 1–4: a copy's own row was its best-matching row for 98.1, 87.3 and 57.5 % of copies). The simulations are star-like and of our own design and serve as a supplement.

### What SubFam gives
A readable, ordered alignment that stands for the collection, every row traceable to its copies, built without an identity cutoff and without a reference. The ablation locates the contribution in the combination of ordering and chunking rather than in either alone. The fixed chunk trades redundancy (several rows for an abundant group, Example 3) for freedom from a cutoff.
### The steps that follow
Groups are defined downstream, on the compact alignment, where the methods that need a small input become applicable: a search for diagnostic columns (a column at which a block of rows is fixed for one base that the other rows lack), a tree or network of the rows, a curated library built from chosen rows and their member copies (`-e`), or a comparison of the chunks with a classification of the user's own (`-g`). Each of these is a method with its own test; this paper describes the preparation of their input. In our own analyses of SINE families the chunk consensuses served as the material from which subfamilies were delimited and their consensuses chosen [AUTHOR: cite only the two or three papers whose Methods name SubFam; candidates: Vassetzky et al. 2021 (already cited for B2), Kosushkin et al. 2022 Can, Ustyantsev et al. 2024 Ere, Borodulina et al. 2025, Ustyantsev et al. 2026].
### Cascaded use
When the copies come from subfamilies of different age and size, one N serves them unequally: a young, abundant subfamily fills many near-identical rows while an old or rare one is diluted across mixed chunks. SubFam can then be run in cascade with sequential depletion: the rows of a first run that the researcher recognises as one group are taken out together with their copies (`-e` writes the copies of chosen chunks; the remainder, built with any FASTA tool, is the next input), and SubFam is run again on what is left with settings adjusted to it, a smaller N for the rare groups, `-c` or a shared region for truncated ones. The removed and remaining copies together account for the whole input, and the rows of all rounds can be aligned together at the end. This is a proposed use of the tool, not benchmarked here, with the grouping decision made by the researcher between rounds.
### Relationship to existing tools
Identity clustering returns one representative per cluster and leaves the clusters unordered; COSEG and Price et al. define subfamilies on copies that span a common window of one reference alignment (full-length copies in Price et al.); TEtrimmer curates a library one family at a time from a genome; PASTA [20] and MAGUS [24] decompose a guide tree in order to align everything, where SubFam collapses the subsets and aligns the consensuses. Each answers a different question; SubFam's ordered consensuses are a compact input for downstream curation and alignment.
### Limitations
A chunk has a fixed size: an abundant group fills several rows with near-identical consensuses, a chunk can straddle two groups, and N has to be chosen for the rarest group of interest. Rows represent old families less closely than young ones. The whole-copy k-mer distance responds to copy length, so mixed-length input (5′-truncated LINEs) sorts partly by length; the remedy is to run on the shared region or on the long copies. The k-mer ordering needs a matrix of 4·n² bytes (3.6 GB at 30,000 copies; the whole run peaked at 4.1–4.3 GB); `-P` needs far less memory (0.7–0.8 GB at 30,000 copies) at a cost in chunk purity (0.400 against 0.472 on real Alu, 0.756 against 0.805 on B2). The labels used to score the examples are annotations or published consensuses, and with another classification the comparison with identity clustering can turn (Table 2, B2). Each real example comes from one genome.

## Conclusions
SubFam turns a collection of related copies that is too large to read into a compact, ordered alignment of chunk consensuses, with every row traceable to its copies, built without an identity cutoff and without a reference. On real SINE and LINE copies the rows carry the consensuses of the young classes (identity 0.98–1.00) and approach those of the old ones (0.89–0.94), and on B2 they show the lineages as blocks. Removing the ordering or the chunking brings chunk purity down to that of a single family consensus; identity clustering of the same copies finds no cutoff at which the groups of the old families are both pure and cover all copies, and on a young family it matches SubFam with part of the copies left in small groups. The output is a compact input for curation, subfamily definition and phylogenetic analysis, which are hard to apply to thousands of raw copies; those steps remain the researcher's.
