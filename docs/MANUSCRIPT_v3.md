## Abstract
**Background.** Transposable-element families, satellite arrays and multigene families can contain thousands to millions of related copies. A person cannot read them, and downstream tools (multiple aligners, tree builders, annotation) cannot be fed all of them. An identity threshold that works for a young family shreds an old one into singletons.
**Results.** SubFam orders the copies along a k-mer guide tree, cuts the order into chunks of N neighbours, calls a plurality consensus for each chunk and aligns the consensuses: a short alignment, ordered by similarity, with a table of the copies behind each row. There is no identity threshold and no reference. On 3,000 real mouse B2 SINE copies it produced 60 rows in which the young subfamilies of Vassetzky et al. (2021) appear as blocks of chunks with their diagnostic indels, and the old ones (about 70 % identity to their own consensus) do not separate, which the alignment shows. On 2,222 human L1PA2–8 copies the 111 rows follow the RepeatMasker age classes at the coarser resolution that whole-copy sequence allows, and at finer resolution when only the 3′ end is used. Identity-threshold clustering on the same copies leaves 60–95 % of old copies as singletons or merges across classes; for young Alu loci, where a threshold exists, it performs as well as SubFam in purity.
**Conclusions.** SubFam bridges manual curation and computational analysis: it organises a pile of raw copies into an ordered, inspectable alignment that downstream tools can take. It does not define subfamilies, does not resolve orphan or truncated copies, and a chunk is not a subfamily.
**Keywords:** transposable elements; SINE; consensus sequences; repeat families; subfamilies; alignment

## Background
Interspersed repeats, satellite arrays and multigene families can contain thousands to millions of related copies. They are too many to inspect and too diverged to group by one identity threshold: a threshold that separates the subfamilies of a young family splits an old one into singletons, and one that suits an old family merges the subfamilies of a young one. Identity-based clustering (CD-HIT, VSEARCH, MMseqs2) returns either a real copy, carrying its own private mutations, or a cluster consensus whose quality depends on where the threshold fell. Methods that define subfamilies statistically, from the whole-genome Alu analysis of Price et al. (2004) to its successor COSEG, need every copy aligned to one reference over a shared window and so set aside truncated copies. Library-curation tools such as TEtrimmer (Qian et al. 2025) work from a genome and from one locus at a time (up to 100 copies per alignment, at most five variant clusters per locus [docs/research/TETRIMMER.md, from the v1.7.4 source]); they curate a library, they do not summarise a given collection of sequences. Annotation assigns each copy its best-scoring library consensus, a label without a stated uncertainty.
Between the golden standard of manual curation and a researcher's computational analysis lies a practical gap: a heap of unorganised raw copies. SubFam fills that gap and does not claim more. It has two objectives. (1) A visually observable presentation of a variety of similar sequences as similarity subgroups. (2) An alignment proxy: a short, ordered alignment of chunk consensuses that stands for thousands of repetitive sequences in tools that cannot take them all. It sets no identity threshold, it does not call subfamilies, a chunk is not a subfamily, and orphan and truncated copies are not resolved.

## Implementation
*[Reused unchanged in substance from MANUSCRIPT_BMC.md, updated to SubFam 1.3.0.]*
**Algorithm.** (1) *Order.* All sequences are placed along a UPGMA tree built from the weighted Jaccard (Ružička) distance of their k-mer counts (default k = 6); at each merge the two subtrees are flipped so that their closest ends meet. No alignment is computed. The tree code is a numpy port of the k-mer tree of ViewAlign. The distance matrix was checked against a brute-force computation and the output is byte-identical across thread counts [docs/research/CODE_REVIEW.md]. (2) *Chunk.* The order is cut into NCHUNK = round(n / N) consecutive blocks of near-equal size (default N = 50); no sequence is dropped. (3) *Consensus.* Each chunk is aligned with MAFFT (in parallel) and a plurality consensus is called, with the scoring of EMBOSS cons (EDNAFULL): a base is written when at least p × n sequences agree (default p = 0.36, the value inherited from our earlier SINE work; bases supported by at most half of the sequences are written in lower case). With `-c` the threshold is p × the number of sequences that span the column (at least 3), so that consensuses of truncated copies extend as far as the data does. (4) *Align.* The chunk consensuses are aligned with MAFFT L-INS-i (`--auto` above 2,000 consensuses). Outputs: the consensuses, their alignment (FASTA, MSF) and a table of copy, chunk and strand.
**Parameter.** N is the resolution in copies: a group needs roughly N copies to obtain a row of its own. There is no identity threshold.
**Limits of the implementation.** The distance matrix is a dense n × n float32 array (400 MB at 10⁴ copies, 2.2 GB at 2 × 10⁴): the k-mer ordering is for up to about 2 × 10⁴ copies; PartTree ordering (`-P`) is the route beyond, not tested in this draft. N characters are deleted before k-mer counting, so neighbouring letters join into spurious k-mers [CODE_REVIEW.md; not fixed].
**Presentation of the examples.** Every example alignment (`examples/`) is one MAFFT alignment of three kinds of row: *class consensuses* (the plurality consensus of the real member copies of a labelled class; `TRUE_` in the current files, to be renamed — it is the best any method could reach given the labels, not the truth), *published consensuses* (Dfam, Price et al. 2004, Vassetzky et al. 2021), and the SubFam rows, each named `<chunk>|<class>|p<purity>`, where purity is the fraction of the chunk's copies in the majority class. Identity of every SubFam row to the head of its group is in `<file>.rows.tsv`. Viewer links in `examples/README.md` open them in ViewAlign.

## Results
### Mouse B2 (Figure 1, Figure 2)
*Data.* Every RepeatMasker B2_Mm1a, B2_Mm1t, B2_Mm2, B3 and B3A copy on chr19 of mm39 (≥ 170 bp, no N, on the element strand): 5,972 copies; a random 3,000 (seed 1) were run with `-n 50`, giving 60 rows in 19 s [benchmark/sine_b2/README.md]. Two classifications of this family exist. Repbase/Dfam names five subfamilies resting on single-nucleotide diagnostics. Vassetzky et al. (2021, IJMS 22:9897) define B2a–B2e by long indels in 92,352 full-length mm10 copies and treat Mm1a + Mm1t + Mm2 as one subfamily (B2a), with Mm1 and Mm2 as extreme cases in a continuum, and B3 as B2d. We took their five consensuses from Figure S2 of the supplement and labelled every copy by its best hit [kramerov_consensus.sh, label_by_consensus.py].
*Chunks against the RepeatMasker classes.* Median identity of the 60 rows to the head of their own class 0.959, minimum 0.836, 5 rows closer to another head. B2_Mm1a rows are clean (purity 0.78–1.00), B2_Mm2 mostly (10 of 14 rows ≥ 0.76), and B3 forms blocks. B2_Mm1t has no rows of its own: its copies lie between Mm1a and Mm2, three of its six rows are closer to a neighbouring head. The chunk order runs B3/B3A, then B2_Mm2 → B2_Mm1t → B2_Mm1a, then B3. B3A, 542 of 3,000 copies, mixes with B3.
*Chunks against the Vassetzky subfamilies.* The cross-table of the two labellings (3,000 copies) shows that B2_Mm1a, B2_Mm1t and 563 of 672 B2_Mm2 copies are B2a, as the 2021 paper says, that 104 B2_Mm2 copies are B2b (no Repbase name), that B3 is mostly B2d (927 of 1,266) with B2e (190) and B2c (141), and that B3A is a mixture of B2d, B2e and B2c rather than a subfamily of the 2021 scheme. Grouped by the 2021 labels, B2a is a block of 22 rows, 20 of them ≥ 0.92 pure; B2b and B2c have two rows each (4–7 % of the copies); B2d takes 34 rows; B2e takes none, its 417 copies are spread over B2d rows. The young, indel-defined subfamilies resolve. B2d and B2e, whose copies are 67–70 % identical to their own consensus (B2c, B2d and B2e consensuses are 77–85 % identical to each other), do not: the copies are as far from each other as from the other subfamily's consensus, the best-hit labels are uncertain there, and the alignment shows the mixing instead of hiding it. That is a limit of any copy-level method on 25–30 % diverged SINEs.
*From chaos to order (Figure 2).* To show what the method does, we hand-picked 390 real copies (B2a 120, B2b 30, B2c 120, B2d 120; shuffled) and aligned them with the 20 SubFam consensuses (`-n 20`) in one alignment. In input order the copies are speckle. In SubFam's order a B2a block with the 11-bp central deletion, a B2c block with its own deletion and diagnostic columns, and the B2b rows between them appear, while B2d stays mixed at both ends. The 20 chunk consensuses carry the same structure. The selection is deliberate and said so in the legend [docs/figures/sketch_chaos_to_order_b2.png; `benchmark/sine_b2/figure_chaos_to_order.py`; the figure is a sketch, to be redrawn].

### Real L1: human L1PA2–8
2,222 RepeatMasker L1PA2–L1PA8 copies ≥ 1 kb on hg38 chr4 (`-n 20 -c`: 111 rows, 6.5 min). L1PA classes are age classes along one lineage and were defined on the 3′ end (Smit et al. 1995; Khan et al. 2006) [benchmark/line_real/README.md §2]. Seven consensuses differ at 8–31 of ~900 aligned positions between neighbours, fewer than the 10–45 private differences of a single copy. Rows against the head of their RepeatMasker class: median identity 0.993, minimum 0.955; 17 of 111 rows closer to another head. Chunk purity against RepeatMasker is 0.696 for whole copies; the chunks combine neighbouring classes (PA2 + PA3, PA4–PA6, PA7 + PA8), which are the ORF2-level classes of Smit's Table 2, and never distant ones. On the last 900 bp only, purity rises to 0.796 and 61 of 111 chunks have purity ≥ 0.9 (from 32). Re-labelling copies by their 3′ end reproduces the RepeatMasker label for 91.6 % of copies, which is the noise floor of this reference. The RepeatMasker labels are used as an age-ordered external reference, not as truth. No subfamily-calling step is applied to the rows.

On the old families no threshold gives a short result that keeps every copy: low thresholds merge across classes (L1 at 0.80: three groups hold 96 % of the copies, purity 0.36, below SubFam's 0.70), high thresholds leave most copies alone (B2 at 0.80: 1,906 of 3,000 copies are singletons). On the young Alu loci a threshold exists, and vsearch at 0.95 matches SubFam in purity (0.94 against 0.92) while covering 89 % of the copies; SubFam's gain there is completeness and an ordered alignment, not purity. Purity rewards small groups, which the coverage column corrects for; N was fixed before looking at labels; labels are RepeatMasker's or Price's, not truth; `cluster_fast` is greedy; one chromosome per family. We did not run COSEG, CD-HIT, or MeShClust. [AUTHOR: decide on COSEG for Alu, or a paragraph only.]

### Simulation (supplement)
Simulations isolate one property real data cannot: the age of the family at known truth. *[Numbers carried from MANUSCRIPT_BMC.md; produced with SubFam 1.2.0 and not re-run with 1.3.0 (1.3.0 changed how a remainder is split into chunks and fixed a floating-point rounding in the consensus threshold; the effect on these numbers was not measured). Re-run `examples/rebuild.sh` and `benchmark/multiseed` before submission.]* With eight subfamilies at 3, 8 and 15 % divergence, SubFam recovered all eight source consensuses in every run at `-n 20` (100 rows for 2,000 copies), whereas the VSEARCH identity that recovers the sources changed with age (0.95 for the young family, 0.70 for the old one) and even the best choice per replicate recovered 5.25 of 8 in the oldest family. A harder simulation (CpG decay, source elements, mixed ages, truncated copies) gave 6.3–7.8 of 8 at 100 rows. Rows are faithful to their copies in young and middle families and less so in old ones (a copy's own row was its best match for 98.1, 87.3 and 57.5 % of copies in the young, middle and old family at `-n 50`) [benchmark/multiseed; examples/sim_*].

## Discussion
**What SubFam gives.** An alignment that a person can read and that a tool can take, with every row traceable to its copies. In B2 it shows the structure that an indel-based classification found by hand (the 11-bp deletion, the 3′ insertions), shows the Mm1–Mm2 continuum as a gradient instead of five labels, and shows where the data give no support for a split (B2d/B2e). SubFam asserts none of this; the rows are data for the reader.
**Integration into downstream analysis (theoretical; no new method).** The chunk-consensus alignment can stand in for the full set in several tasks, each of which would need its own test: (i) a phylogeny or network of subfamilies, from a tree of the 20–200 consensuses rather than thousands of copies; (ii) a starting point for library curation, where the rows are candidate variants whose members are known; (iii) diagnostic-position discovery, since a position that separates blocks of rows is a candidate marker for a subfamily; (iv) profile-HMM or consensus libraries for annotation, with the caution that a chunk is not a subfamily; (v) satellite arrays and multigene families, where the order itself shows higher-order structure. [AUTHOR: cite your own past work for (i)–(v); one concrete case, a tree of the 60 B2 chunk consensuses, can be added if reviewers ask.]
**Relationship to existing tools.** Threshold clustering returns a representative per cluster and has no order; COSEG and the Price et al. approach define subfamilies on aligned, full-length copies; TEtrimmer curates a library per locus from a genome and cannot take a FASTA of copies; progressive aligners such as PASTA and MAGUS merge subset alignments to align everything, where SubFam collapses subsets and aligns the consensuses [docs/research/RELATED_WORK.md]. They answer different questions. SubFam is the step before: the organised view.
**Limitations.** A chunk has a fixed size, so an abundant subfamily fills several rows and a chunk can straddle two; N must be chosen for the rarest group of interest. Rows are less faithful in old families. Truncated long copies are ordered poorly by whole-sequence k-mer distance (`-c` lets consensuses extend; fragments are not placed). Memory is quadratic (practical limit about 2 × 10⁴ copies without `-P`). The class labels used throughout are annotations or best hits, not independent truth. The B2 and L1 results are one chromosome of one genome each.
