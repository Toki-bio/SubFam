## Abstract
**Background.** Transposable-element families, satellite arrays and multigene families contain thousands to millions of related copies. A person cannot read them, and the tools that come next (multiple aligners, tree builders, library curation) cannot take them all. An identity threshold that suits a young family shreds an old one into singletons, and one that suits an old family merges the groups of a young one.
**Results.** SubFam orders the copies along a k-mer guide tree, cuts the order into chunks of N neighbours, calls a plurality consensus for each chunk and aligns the consensuses. The output is a short alignment, ordered by similarity, with a table of the copies behind every row; no reference and no identity threshold are needed, and N is the only parameter that matters. On 3,000 mouse B2 SINE copies it gives 60 rows in 19 s in which the known structure of the family is visible; on 2,222 5′-truncated human L1PA2–8 copies it gives 111 rows that follow the age classes at the resolution the whole copies allow; on 316 polymorphic Alu loci, 16 rows. Removing any one step (the ordering, the chunking, the consensus) loses the result: chunks of unordered copies are no better than one family-wide consensus, and without the consensus step the researcher is left with the 3,000-row alignment the method exists to avoid. Identity-threshold clustering of the same copies either leaves most copies as singletons or merges across groups; on a young family it matches SubFam only in purity, not in completeness. With tree-partitioning (PartTree) ordering, 20,000 copies take three minutes and 0.2 GB.
**Conclusions.** SubFam organises a pile of raw copies into an ordered, inspectable alignment that a person can read and a downstream tool can take. It does not define subfamilies: a chunk is not a subfamily, and orphan and truncated copies are not resolved.
**Keywords:** transposable elements; repeat families; consensus sequences; multiple sequence alignment; visualisation

## Background
Interspersed repeats, satellite arrays and multigene families contain thousands to millions of related copies. They are too many to inspect and too diverged to group by one identity threshold. Identity-based clustering (CD-HIT, VSEARCH, MMseqs2, MeShClust) returns either a real copy, carrying its own private mutations, or a cluster consensus whose content depends on where the threshold fell, and leaves copies below the threshold alone. Methods that define subfamilies statistically, from the whole-genome Alu analysis of Price et al. (2004) to COSEG, need every copy aligned to one reference over a shared window and set truncated copies aside. Library-curation tools such as TEtrimmer work from a genome and one locus at a time [docs/research/TETRIMMER.md]. Annotation assigns each copy its best-scoring library consensus, a label without a stated uncertainty.
Between the golden standard of manual curation and a researcher's computational analysis lies a practical gap: a heap of unorganised raw copies. SubFam fills that gap and does not claim more. It has two objectives. (1) A visually observable presentation of a variety of similar sequences as similarity subgroups. (2) An alignment proxy: a short, ordered alignment of chunk consensuses that stands for thousands of repetitive sequences in tools that cannot take them all. It sets no identity threshold, it does not call subfamilies, a chunk is not a subfamily, and orphan and truncated copies are not resolved.

## Implementation
**Algorithm.** (1) *Order.* All sequences are placed along a UPGMA tree built from the weighted Jaccard (Ružička) distance of their k-mer counts (default k = 6); at each merge the two subtrees are flipped so that their closest ends meet, which turns the tree into a sequence in which neighbours are similar across subtree boundaries. No alignment is computed. The distance matrix was checked against a brute-force computation and the output is byte-identical across thread counts [docs/research/CODE_REVIEW.md]. (2) *Chunk.* The order is cut into round(n / N) consecutive blocks of near-equal size (default N = 50); no sequence is dropped. (3) *Consensus.* Each chunk is aligned with MAFFT (chunks in parallel) and a plurality consensus is called with the scoring of EMBOSS cons (EDNAFULL): the top-scoring residue of a column is written when its positive-scoring matches reach p × n (default p = 0.36; bases supported by at most half of the sequences are written in lower case, and the lower case is kept through the final alignment). With `-c` the threshold is p × the number of sequences that span the column (at least 3), so that consensuses of truncated copies extend as far as the data does. (4) *Align.* The chunk consensuses are aligned with MAFFT L-INS-i (`--auto` above 2,000 consensuses). Outputs: the consensuses, their alignment (FASTA, MSF), a table of copy, chunk and strand, and a label-free table of chunk statistics (size, consensus length, share of weakly supported bases, agreement of the members with the consensus, lowest member identity, gap share). Two options return to the original loci: `-e` writes the original sequences of chosen chunks to separate FASTA files; `-g` reports the composition of every chunk by groups the user supplies. Mixed-strand input is handled by `-r` (strand-independent k-mers, each copy oriented like its neighbour). SubFam does not define groups; how chunks are grouped (diagnostic columns, a tree cut, annotation) is left to the next step.
**Parameter.** N is the resolution in copies: a group needs roughly N copies to obtain a row of its own. On the B2 example purity against annotation labels moves by 0.05 over N = 20–100, by at most 0.04 over k = 4–10, not at all with p (p changes the consensus, not the chunks), and by 0.02 between random samples of the copies [benchmark/sine_b2/sensitivity.tsv]. There is no identity threshold.
**Scale.** The k-mer ordering holds a dense n × n float32 matrix (0.8 GB at 10,000 copies, 2.2 GB at 20,000); the script warns above 20,000, and PartTree ordering (`-P`) removes the matrix: 10,000 and 20,000 simulated copies took 65 and 173 s at 104 and 194 MB against 75 and 247 s at 839 and 2,216 MB for the k-mer path, with all eight simulated sources recovered either way and chunk purity 0.89–0.90 against 0.92–0.95 [docs/SCALING.md].
**Equivalence and tests.** The consensus and MSF writers reproduce EMBOSS cons and seqret byte for byte [tests/check_equivalence.sh]; the consensus and chunk-statistics code was checked against independent reimplementations on 8,000 random alignments under two awk implementations, and the chunk splitter for every input size from 1 to 30 against N from 2 to 10 (2026-10-11 code review; [AUTHOR: add as tests/ or cite the review file]).
**Presentation of the examples.** Every example alignment (`examples/`) is one MAFFT alignment of three kinds of row: *class consensuses* (`CLASSCONS_<class>`: the plurality consensus of the real member copies of a labelled class, the best any method could reach given the labels, not the truth), *published consensuses* (Dfam, Price et al. 2004), and the SubFam rows, each named `<chunk>|<class>|p<purity>`, purity being the fraction of the chunk's copies in its majority class. Labels (RepeatMasker, published subfamilies) are used only to colour and to score, never by the method. Identity of every SubFam row to the head of its group is in `<file>.rows.tsv`.

## Results
### Example 1: mouse B2 SINEs (Figure 1, Figure 2)
Every RepeatMasker B2_Mm1a, B2_Mm1t, B2_Mm2, B3 and B3A copy on chr19 of mm39 (≥ 170 bp, no N, on the element strand) was taken: 5,972 copies; a random 3,000 (seed 1) were run with `-n 50`, giving 60 rows in 19 s [benchmark/sine_b2/README.md]. Grouped by the RepeatMasker class with each class's member consensus and the Dfam consensus as heads (Figure 1): median identity of the 60 rows to the head of their own class 0.959, minimum 0.836; 5 rows are closer to another head. The k-mer order separates the B3 and B2 lineages into blocks of chunks (B3/B3A chunks 1–21 and 46–60, B2 chunks 22–45); inside the B2 block the three Repbase names interleave, and B2_Mm1t, whose consensus differs from B2_Mm1a at 2 of 193 positions, has no rows of its own. The old B3A class (542 copies) mixes with B3. These are what the alignment shows; SubFam asserts nothing about the subfamilies, and whether the Repbase names or an indel-based classification of the family (Vassetzky et al. 2021) fit the copies better is a question for another paper [AUTHOR: co-author of that classification; the comparison is in the supplement, `benchmark/sine_b2/README.md`].
*From chaos to order (Figure 2).* To show what the method does, 390 of the copies (a stratified random sample over four groups of the family, seed 1, shuffled) were aligned together with the 20 chunk consensuses SubFam built from them (`-n 20`). In input order the copies are speckle; in SubFam's order the groups appear as blocks with their shared indels, and the 20 consensuses carry the same structure in 20 rows [docs/figures/sketch_chaos_to_order_b2.png; `benchmark/sine_b2/figure_chaos_to_order.py`].
*Limit.* The oldest copies of the family (about 70 % identical to their own consensus) do not form blocks of their own at N = 50: their chunks mix, and the alignment shows the mixing instead of hiding it. That is the limit of any copy-level method on 25–30 % diverged SINEs.

### Example 2: 5′-truncated L1 copies (Supplementary Figure 1)
2,222 RepeatMasker L1PA2–L1PA8 copies of at least 1 kb on hg38 chr4 (mean length 3.3 kb; over 90 % of L1 copies are 5′-truncated), run with `-n 20 -c`: 111 rows in 6 min 31 s on 8 threads [benchmark/line_real/README.md]. The rows reach as far 5′ as their copies do (`-c`), and against the head of their RepeatMasker class they have median identity 0.993, minimum 0.955; 17 of 111 rows are closer to a neighbouring class's head. The L1PA classes are consecutive age classes of one lineage whose consensuses differ at 8–31 of ~900 positions, and the chunks follow them at the coarser resolution of neighbouring classes (PA2 + PA3, PA4–PA6, PA7 + PA8). *Limit.* The whole-copy k-mer distance is sensitive to copy length: chunks are nearly length-homogeneous (within-chunk length CV 0.05 against 0.59 for random chunks), so one class is split over several rows by length, and the tree built from the 3′ region every copy shares differs from the whole-copy tree [benchmark/line_real/README.md §4–5, Supplementary Table]. For 5′-truncated families the recommended use is to run SubFam on the region the copies share, or on the long copies, and to place the fragments afterwards.

### Example 3: young Alu loci (Supplementary Figure 2)
316 Sanger-sequenced polymorphic Alu loci (Konkel et al. 2015), cut to the Alu body, `-n 20`: 16 rows; the Price et al. (2004) consensuses for AluY, AluYa5 and AluYb8 sit at the head of their groups with median identity of rows to head 1.000, minimum 0.996 [examples/konkel_alu_n20.rows.tsv]. Seven of the 16 rows are distinct sequences: in a young family several chunks of one group give the same consensus, which is the price of fixed chunks (Discussion). The references are the consensuses that labelled these copies, so this is an illustration, not a validation. A genome-wide Alu run is [NOT RUN: `benchmark/alu_hg38/`, on the author's servers].

### What each step contributes
Each step of SubFam exists elsewhere: guide-tree leaf orders (MAFFT `--reorder`), the decomposition of a guide tree into subsets (PASTA, UPP), family consensus builders (RepeatModeler's Refiner), per-cluster consensuses (VSEARCH `--consout`). None of them produces a short, ordered alignment with a provenance table and no threshold, and the ablation shows that the object is made by the combination [benchmark/ablation/README.md]. On the 3,000 B2 copies with N = 50 (purity against the RepeatMasker label; the label-free chunk statistics rank the variants the same way):

| variant | rows | columns | purity |
|---|---|---|---|
| SubFam (k-mer order with closest-ends flip, chunks, consensus, alignment) | 60 | 223 | 0.79 |
| no ordering (shuffled input, same chunks) | 60 | 237 | 0.43 |
| no ordering (genomic order) | 60 | 242 | 0.43 |
| MAFFT guide-tree order instead of the k-mer order | 60 | 223 | 0.73 |
| k-mer UPGMA order without the closest-ends flip | 60 | 234 | 0.77 |
| no chunks: one consensus of all copies | 1 | 194 | 0.42 |
| no consensus: MAFFT alignment of all 3,000 copies | 3,000 | 2,781 | – |

Chunks of unordered copies are at the level of one family consensus; the generic guide-tree order gives most of the gain, the k-mer order with the flip the rest; without the consensus step the researcher has the 3,000-row alignment.

### Against identity-threshold clustering (Table 1)
`vsearch --cluster_fast` and MeShClust2 (mean-shift assignment with alignment-free identity; Girgis lab) at several identities, against the SubFam chunks and one family-wide consensus [benchmark/baseline_vsearch/README.md, benchmark/baseline_meshclust/README.md]. Purity is the fraction of copies carrying the majority label of their group; a "real" group has at least 10 copies; distinct rows are counted after collapsing identical consensus sequences.

| example | method | groups | singletons | copies in groups ≥ 10 | purity of those |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| B2, 3,000 copies | one consensus | 1 | 0 | 100 % | 0.42 |
| | **SubFam N = 50** (60 distinct rows) | **60** | **0** | **100 %** | **0.79** |
| | vsearch 0.60 / 0.80 / 0.95 | 632 / 1,992 / 2,868 | 389 / 1,906 / 2,812 | 63 / 29 / 1 % | 0.67 / 0.80 / 0.82 |
| | MeShClust2 0.60 / 0.80 / 0.95 | 636 / 2,328 / 2,882 | 533 / 2,233 / 2,865 | 73 / 16 / 3 % | 0.46 / 0.56 / 0.95 |
| L1PA2–8, 2,222 copies | one consensus | 1 | 0 | 100 % | 0.22 |
| | **SubFam N = 20, -c** (111 distinct rows) | **111** | **0** | **100 %** | **0.70** |
| | vsearch 0.80 / 0.90 / 0.95 | 45 / 753 / 1,557 | 31 / 686 / 1,475 | 96 / 60 / 22 % | 0.36 / 0.48 / 0.83 |
| | MeShClust2 0.80 / 0.90 / 0.95 | 317 / 709 / 1,992 | 246 / 639 / 1,957 | 81 / 66 / 7 % | 0.27 / 0.34 / 0.65 |
| Alu loci, 316 copies | one consensus | 1 | 0 | 100 % | 0.52 |
| | **SubFam N = 20** (7 distinct of 16 rows) | **16** | **0** | **100 %** | **0.92** |
| | vsearch 0.95 / 0.98 / 0.99 | 18 / 70 / 140 | 8 / 38 / 101 | 89 / 65 / 35 % | 0.94 / 0.97 / 0.98 |
| | MeShClust2 0.90 / 0.95 / 0.98 | 1 / 11 / 40 | 0 / 4 / 24 | 100 / 95 / 78 % | 0.52 / 0.77 / 0.86 |

With either tool, on the old families no threshold gives a short result that keeps every copy: low thresholds merge across classes (L1 at 0.80: three groups hold 96 % of the copies at purity 0.36), high thresholds leave most copies alone (B2 at 0.80: 1,906 of 3,000 are singletons). On the young Alu loci a threshold exists and vsearch at 0.95 matches SubFam in purity (0.94 against 0.92) while covering 89 % of the copies; there SubFam's gain is completeness and the order, not purity. Caveats: purity rewards small groups (hence the coverage column); the labels are annotations, not truth; `cluster_fast` is greedy; one chromosome per family. COSEG, run once on the Konkel loci, separated AluYb8 but not AluYa5 from AluY and at matched granularity scored above SubFam's fixed chunks on V-measure, which over-split by design [benchmark/alu_konkel/README.md].

### Simulation (Supplementary Figure 3)
Simulations give the one thing real data cannot, a known truth across family ages. With eight subfamilies at 3, 8 and 15 % divergence (eight replicates each), SubFam `-n 20` recovered all eight source consensuses in every run with 100 rows for 2,000 copies, whereas the VSEARCH identity that recovers the sources changed with age (0.90–0.95 for the young family, 0.70 for the old one) and even the best identity chosen per replicate with the truth recovered 5.25 of 8 in the old family [benchmark/multiseed]. In a harder simulation (CpG decay, source elements, mixed ages, truncated copies) SubFam recovered 6.3–7.8 of 8 at 100 rows when identity is measured over non-CpG columns; over all columns CpG decay keeps nearly every consensus of every method below 99 % identity to the master [benchmark/hard]. In the simple simulation 10 of 40 rows at N = 50 are distinct: an abundant subfamily fills several chunks with the same consensus. Rows are faithful to their copies in young and middle families and less so in old ones (a copy's own row was its best-matching row for 98.1, 87.3 and 57.5 % of copies). The simulations are star-like and of our own design; they are a supplement, not the evidence.

## Discussion
**What SubFam gives.** A readable, ordered alignment that stands for the collection, with every row traceable to its copies, built without a threshold and without a reference. The ablation locates the contribution: not any step, which all exist, but their combination into one object; and the fixed chunk, which trades redundancy (several rows for an abundant group) for the absence of a threshold.
**What comes next, and is not in SubFam.** Groups are defined downstream. The chunk-consensus alignment is small enough for the steps that need a small input: a diagnostic-column search over the rows (a column where a block of rows is fixed for one base that the other rows do not carry), a tree or network of the rows, a curated library from chosen rows with their member copies (`-e`), or a comparison of the chunks with a classification the user supplies (`-g`). Each of these is a separate method with its own test, and none is claimed here. [AUTHOR: cite your own past uses of this workflow if wanted.]
**Relationship to existing tools.** Threshold clustering returns a representative per cluster and has no order; COSEG and Price et al. define subfamilies on full-length copies aligned to one reference; TEtrimmer curates a library per locus from a genome; PASTA and MAGUS decompose a guide tree to align everything, where SubFam collapses the subsets and aligns the consensuses [docs/research/RELATED_WORK.md]. They answer different questions; SubFam is the step before them.
**Limitations.** A chunk has a fixed size: an abundant group fills several rows with near-identical consensuses, a chunk can straddle two groups, and N must be chosen for the rarest group of interest. Rows are less faithful in old families. The whole-copy k-mer distance is sensitive to copy length, so mixed-length input (5′-truncated LINEs) sorts partly by length; run on the shared region or on the long copies. Memory is quadratic without `-P`. The labels used to score the examples are annotations or published consensuses, not independent truth, and each real example is one chromosome of one genome.

SubFam is a bash script with embedded Python (numpy ≥ 1.20) and awk; MAFFT ≥ 7.5; MIT; https://github.com/Toki-bio/SubFam.
