## Introduction
Interspersed repeats, satellite arrays and multigene families can contain tens of thousands to millions of related copies. They are too many to inspect and too diverged to group by one identity threshold: a threshold that separates the subfamilies of a young family splits an old one into singletons, and one that suits an old family merges the subfamilies of a young one. Tools that cluster by identity (CD-HIT, VSEARCH, MMseqs2) return either a real copy, which carries all of its own private mutations, or a cluster consensus whose quality depends on where the threshold fell. Methods that define subfamilies statistically, from Price et al.'s whole-genome Alu analysis to its successor COSEG, require every copy to be aligned to one reference over a shared window, and so set aside copies that are truncated or rearranged.
The practical need that remains is smaller than subfamily definition: to bring an unorganised multitude into an order a person can read. SubFam does that and nothing more. It orders the copies by k-mer similarity, averages blocks of neighbours into consensuses, and aligns the consensuses, giving a short alignment in which the variants shared by a group stay visible and the private mutations of single copies are averaged away. A table records which copies built each row, so nothing is hidden. It makes no subfamily calls and does not place individual orphan copies.

## Methods
**Algorithm.** (1) Order. All sequences are placed along a guide tree built from k-mer counts (default k = 6, 3-12 allowed): the distance of two copies is the weighted Jaccard distance of their k-mer counts, the tree is built by UPGMA with the tie rules of ViewAlign's kmer-tree.js (of which SubFam.sh embeds a numpy port; the order is identical to the JavaScript according to tests/check_equivalence.sh), and at every merge the two subtrees are flipped so that their closest ends meet. No alignment is computed in this step. Alternatives: the MAFFT guide tree (`-m`), or MAFFT PartTree (`-P`) for inputs above about 20,000 copies. With `-r`, k-mers are strand-independent and each copy is oriented like its predecessor in the order.
(2) Chunk. The order is cut into blocks of N consecutive sequences (default 50); the remainder (fewer than N) joins the last block, so no sequence is dropped. (3) Consensus. Each chunk is aligned with MAFFT (chunks in parallel) and a plurality consensus is called: a base is written when at least p x n of the n sequences agree (default p = 0.36), using the EDNAFULL scoring rules of EMBOSS cons, reimplemented in awk (byte-identical to cons over 3,600 test runs, per tests/check_equivalence.sh). Columns without a consensus are dropped (`-a` keeps them as N). With `-c` the threshold is p x (sequences that span the column, internal gaps counted, end gaps not), with at least 3 spanning sequences, so consensuses of truncated copies extend as far as the data does. (4) Align. The chunk consensuses are aligned with MAFFT L-INS-i (`--localpair --maxiterate 1000 --ep 0.123`). Outputs: PREFIX.cons.fasta, PREFIX.aln.fasta, PREFIX.msf (an awk writer matching EMBOSS seqret), PREFIX.chunks.tsv (copy, chunk, strand).
**Parameters.** N is the resolution, in copies: a subfamily needs roughly N copies in the input to obtain a row of its own. There is no identity threshold.
**Implementation.** One bash script with embedded awk and python/numpy programs; requires MAFFT, awk and python3 with numpy; EMBOSS is not needed. The n x n distance matrix is float32, so the k-mer ordering is for inputs of about 20,000 copies or fewer; PartTree removes this limit.
**Simulations.** (a) Simple: benchmark/simulate.py. A random 300 bp ancestor evolves along a balanced tree to eight subfamilies (4 diagnostic substitutions per branch, so sisters differ at 8 sites); the subfamilies have 600, 400, 300, 250, 200, 150, 60 and 40 copies; each copy decays independently from its subfamily master by 3, 8 or 15 % (85 % substitutions, 15 % short indels). Eight seeds per scenario [benchmark/multiseed]. (b) Harder: benchmark/hard/simulate2.py, adding a CpG-rich ancestor with CpG decay at 6 times the rate of other sites, 1-5 source elements per subfamily, a per-copy age drawn around the scenario mean, transition bias, and 5'-truncated copies; scenarios young, middle, old, mixed ages (four young and four old subfamilies), and 80 % truncated copies; four seeds [benchmark/hard]. (c) L1-like: benchmark/hard/simulate_line.py (see Results, truncated long copies).
**Scoring.** A subfamily is recovered when an output row whose copies are mostly that subfamily matches the true master at >= 99 % identity (and, in the harder simulations, covers >= 95 % of it); the harder simulations report identity over all columns and over non-CpG columns, because CpG decay moves every consensus away from the master at CpG sites. Compared methods: VSEARCH `--cluster_fast --consout` at identity 0.95 to 0.65, CD-HIT-EST with a consensus per cluster, MMseqs2 centroids [benchmark/results.tsv]; the multi-seed and harder runs use VSEARCH.
**Real data.** [PENDING] hg38 Alu copies (full length, 250-330 bp) from the UCSC RepeatMasker table, three random samples of 30,000 copies; recovery of the Dfam Alu families and the 213 consensuses of Price et al. (2004), at identity 0.95, 0.98 and 0.99 with 90 % coverage, stratified by the number of copies the sample is expected to contain; baselines VSEARCH centroids and SubFam `-m` [benchmark/alu_hg38, protocol fixed before any run].

## Results
**R1. Recovery across family ages, without an identity threshold** [benchmark/multiseed]. In the simple simulation SubFam `-n 20` recovered all eight source consensuses in all 24 runs (three ages, eight seeds) with 100 rows for 2,000 copies; `-n 50` (40 rows) averaged 8.0, 7.9 and 7.4 of 8 for the young, middle and old family and lost the smallest subfamilies (40 and 60 copies, below N) in some runs. The identity at which VSEARCH recovers the sources depends on the age of the family: 0.95 or 0.90 for the young family, 0.85 or 0.80 for the middle one, 0.70 for the old one, needing 92 to 1,278 rows in the young and middle families and about 540 in the old one, where even a threshold chosen for each replicate using the truth recovered 5.25 of 8 on average.
**R2. A harder simulation lowers recovery but not the advantage** [benchmark/hard, 4 seeds]. With CpG decay, source elements, age mixture and truncated copies, SubFam `-n 20` recovered (non-CpG identity) 7.75, 7.5 and 6.25 of 8 in the young, middle and old families, 7.0 in the mixed-age set and 6.5 with 80 % truncated copies (7.25 with `-c`), always with 100 rows; the best fixed VSEARCH identity recovered 7.75, 7.5, 5.5, 6.0 and 4.75 with 50 to 1,000 rows. Identity over all columns was below 99 % for nearly all consensuses of every method, because of CpG decay. `-c` changed nothing at 30 % truncation and helped at 80 %; the MAFFT guide-tree order failed with truncated copies (0.75 of 8) where the k-mer tree did not.
**R3. How well rows stand for their copies** [benchmark/multiseed/fidelity.py, seeds 1-4]. A copy's own row was its best-matching row for 98.1, 87.3 and 57.5 % of the copies of the young, middle and old family (`-n 50`; median identity to the own row 96.7, 91.0 and 82.6 %; a copy is nearest its true master in essentially 100 % of the cases). Fidelity falls with age because chunks mix sister subfamilies (chunk purity 0.99, 0.91, 0.71). A chunk-level cohesion statistic (fraction of members agreeing with the consensus in core columns) separated pure from mixed chunks with an AUC of 0.92 in one simulation [benchmark/hard/chunk_stats.py; prototype].
**R4. Scale** [docs/SCALING.md; not re-run for this draft]. With PartTree ordering and N = 50, 100,000 simulated copies took 30 minutes on four cores (ordering 6 min 14 s, chunk alignments 6 min 12 s, final alignment of 2,000 consensuses 17 min 35 s) and recovered all eight subfamilies at 100 % identity (chunk purity 0.944). The k-mer ordering needs 2.2 GB at 20,000 copies and is n^2 beyond that; the final L-INS-i is quadratic in the number of rows.

## Discussion
SubFam turns a collection too large to read into a short alignment that can be read, and keeps the provenance of every row. The evidence supports a narrow claim: without an identity threshold, rows contain the known consensuses of families of any age in simulation, with far fewer rows than identity clustering needs, and the approach runs on 10^5 copies on a workstation. It does not support more.
It does not call subfamilies: chunks have a fixed size, so an abundant subfamily fills several rows and a chunk can straddle two. It does not resolve orphan copies: a copy far from every row is still listed in the chunk table but is not placed better. Row fidelity falls with family age. N is a parameter, in copies, not an identity, and it has to be chosen for the rarest group of interest. Truncated long copies (LINEs) are ordered poorly by whole-sequence k-mer distance; `-c` lets consensuses extend, and the recommended route is to discover rows from the long copies and place the fragments afterwards [R5]. The simulations are of our own design and not calibrated to a genome.
Related approaches: Price et al. (2004) and COSEG define subfamilies from linked mutations in alignments to one reference and set aside truncated copies (the Dfam curation guidelines recommend visual review and say COSEG is not appropriate where the structure looks truncated); annotation pipelines assign each copy the best-scoring library consensus, a label with no stated uncertainty (Carey et al. 2020: more than 10 % of replicate Alu pairs are labelled differently). SubFam addresses a different task: to make the multitude readable so that such analyses, or a person, can start from a short alignment.
